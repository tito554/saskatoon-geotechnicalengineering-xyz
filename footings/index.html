Out here on the prairies, you learn fast that not all flat ground is equal. Saskatoon sits on a complex mix of glacial Lake Agassiz sediments—till, stratified drift, and those tricky lacustrine clays near the riverbank. When we look at a lot for a commercial building or a multi-family walk-up in Stonebridge or Brighton, the first question isn't about floor plans. It's about what's under the topsoil. A shallow foundation can be an efficient, cost-effective solution, but only if the bearing stratum within 1.5 to 3 meters has the stiffness and consistency to handle the column loads without excessive settlement. We routinely pair our site investigation with grain-size analysis to classify those silty tills, and for fine-grained soils, we run Atterberg limits to nail down the plasticity index—critical when you're dealing with the high-plasticity clays that show up in the Sutherland and Nutana areas.

In Saskatoon, the difference between a 1.2 m and a 2.0 m footing depth can be the difference between a stiff glacial till and a compressible lacustrine clay—our design makes that call with data, not guesswork.

Technical details of the service in Saskatoon

Saskatoon's growth since the 1960s has pushed development into zones where the soil profile shifts dramatically over a few hundred meters. You can go from dense, overconsolidated Floral till on the east side to softer alluvial deposits near the South Saskatchewan River. Because of this patchwork geology, we never copy-paste a footing design from one block to the next. Our approach ties field data directly to the geotechnical model: we use SPT drilling to measure N-values at footing elevation, then calibrate those numbers against undisturbed samples tested for shear strength. For projects where the client wants to keep excavation depth minimal, we evaluate the feasibility of a mat foundation as an alternative to isolated footings—especially useful on sites where the competent till dips below 2.5 meters and you'd rather spread the load than chase it deeper. Every design package includes settlement calculations under serviceability limit states, not just ultimate bearing capacity, because in a city with expansive clay lenses, differential movement is what keeps building owners up at night.

Typical technical challenges in Saskatoon

The National Building Code of Canada (NBCC 2020) puts the onus on the design engineer to demonstrate that both ultimate and serviceability limit states are satisfied. In Saskatoon, this gets real when you hit a pocket of soft, normally consolidated clay lensing within the till. If the footing bears partly on stiff material and partly on softer stuff, you get differential settlement that cracks partition walls and binds doors—and that's a callback nobody wants. We've seen it happen on Rosewood infill lots where the pre-construction grading plan didn't account for the paleotopography of the till surface. Another risk that flies under the radar is frost heave in silty soils: if the footing isn't carried below 1.8 m, ice lens growth can lift the perimeter and cause structural distress over a single winter. Our design reports explicitly flag these local hazards and specify subgrade preparation protocols—including proof rolling and moisture conditioning—so the contractor has clear, actionable instructions.

Our services

Every shallow foundation we design in Saskatoon starts with a site-specific geotechnical investigation that meets the requirements of NBCC Article 4.2.4.1. We don't just hand you a bearing pressure number; we provide a comprehensive geotechnical report that includes excavation recommendations, soil parameters, and construction quality control steps. Here's how the process typically breaks down:

Bearing Capacity and Settlement Analysis

Using SPT N-values, laboratory shear strength data (UU triaxial or unconfined compression), and consolidation test results, we calculate the allowable bearing pressure for strip, square, or rectangular footings. We check both immediate (elastic) settlement and long-term consolidation settlement, providing the structural engineer with modulus of subgrade reaction values for mat foundation design when needed.

Construction Inspection and Subgrade Verification

Before the concrete goes in, our field technicians inspect the footing subgrade to confirm that the soil conditions match the design assumptions. We perform in-situ density tests, hand penetrometer checks, and visual classification per ASTM D2488. If soft spots or organic material are encountered, we issue a non-conformance report with remediation instructions—typically over-excavation and engineered fill replacement compacted to 98% standard Proctor density.

Top questions

What's the typical price range for a shallow foundation design package for a single commercial building in Saskatoon?

For a standalone commercial structure (e.g., a one-story retail unit or small office), a complete shallow foundation design package—including the site investigation, laboratory testing, bearing capacity analysis, and a signed geotechnical report—typically runs between CA$2,460 and CA$3,900. The final cost depends on the number of boreholes required and the extent of lab testing. A larger multi-tenant plaza or a building with a basement will push toward the upper end because we need more data points to cover the footprint.

How deep do footings need to be in Saskatoon to get below the frost line?

Saskatoon's building bylaw requires a minimum footing depth of 1.8 meters (about 6 feet) for frost protection. However, the geotechnical design depth may be deeper if the bearing soil at 1.8 m is too weak. We often specify 2.1 to 2.4 m in areas with soft lacustrine clay to reach competent glacial till. The depth is always the greater of the frost requirement and the depth to an adequate bearing stratum.

Can you design a shallow foundation if the site has a high water table near the South Saskatchewan River?

Yes, but it requires careful handling. In riverside neighborhoods like Nutana or River Heights, the groundwater table can be within 2 meters of the surface. We design the footing with the buoyant unit weight of the soil and check for potential seepage into the excavation. The report will include dewatering recommendations—typically a sump pump system during construction—and we may specify a mud slab (blinding concrete) to keep the footing base clean and dry before pouring.

What's the difference between a shallow foundation and a deep foundation, and how do I know which one my Saskatoon lot needs?

A shallow foundation transfers building loads to the soil within about 3 meters of the ground surface—typically strip footings, pad footings, or a mat. A deep foundation (piles or piers) goes deeper to bypass weak upper soils. In Saskatoon, if we find dense glacial till with SPT N-values above 15 at a depth of 1.8 to 2.5 m, shallow foundations are usually the economical choice. If the till is deeper than 3 m or the upper soils are very soft (N < 5), we'll recommend a deep foundation. The geotechnical investigation answers this question definitively for your specific lot.

How long does it take to get a shallow foundation design report after the site investigation?

Once the field work is complete—typically one to two days of drilling for a standard commercial lot—we move the soil samples to our ISO 17025 accredited lab. Routine classification and strength tests take about 5 to 7 business days. Consolidation tests, if needed for settlement analysis, add another 3 to 5 days. We deliver the signed geotechnical report with the foundation design recommendations within 10 to 14 business days after the field work wraps up, assuming no unusual soil conditions are found.
